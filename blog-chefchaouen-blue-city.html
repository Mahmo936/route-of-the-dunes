Chefchaouen: A Guide to Morocco's Blue City

Blue-painted alleys in the Rif Mountains, a few hours from Fes — not on our desert route, but a common add-on for a longer Morocco trip.

6 min read · Destinations

Chefchaouen sits apart from almost everything else on a typical Morocco itinerary — tucked into the Rif Mountains in the north, its medina painted almost entirely in shades of blue. It's not on either of our desert routes, but it comes up often enough in trip planning that it deserves an honest answer: what it actually is, and whether it's worth building extra days around.

What Chefchaouen actually is

Founded in 1471 as a small fortress town, Chefchaouen grew into a mountain refuge for Andalusian and Jewish families over the centuries that followed. The blue paint that now defines the town is a more recent tradition than most visitors assume — it became widespread mainly from the 1930s onward, and today it's maintained as much for tourism as for any of the original symbolic reasons often cited for it. Whatever its origin, the effect is real: narrow stepped alleys, every wall and stair washed in overlapping shades of blue, are genuinely unlike anywhere else in Morocco.

What to actually do there

  • Wander the medina without a fixed plan. This is the main activity, and it's enough — the blue streets themselves are the attraction, not any single sight inside them.
  • Plaza Uta el-Hammam. The main square, with the old kasbah and a cluster of cafés — a natural place to start or end a walk through the medina.
  • The Spanish Mosque viewpoint. A short uphill walk outside town gives the classic overview shot of Chefchaouen's blue rooftops against the mountains, especially good near sunset.
  • Ras el-Maa. A small waterfall at the edge of the medina where locals still do laundry — a quieter, more everyday corner of the town.
  • Wool and textiles. Chefchaouen is known regionally for handwoven wool blankets and clothing, sold in shops throughout the medina at generally lower pressure than Marrakech's souks.
Photography tip. Morning light (before the medina fills up) gives the clearest, least crowded shots of the blue alleys — by midday, both the light and the crowds flatten the effect that makes photos of Chefchaouen distinctive in the first place.

Does it fit with a desert tour?

Honestly, not directly. Chefchaouen sits in the far north, while our routes run between Marrakesh, Fes and the Sahara in the center and south of the country — combining both regions in one trip means treating them as two separate legs rather than one continuous route. If you have the time for it, Chefchaouen pairs naturally with the Fes portion of a longer trip, since the two cities are roughly 4 hours apart by road. Practical basics

Most visitors reach Chefchaouen from Fes (around 4 hours by road) or from Tangier and Tetouan if arriving from the north. A single full day is genuinely enough to see the medina properly; an overnight adds a quieter evening and morning in the blue streets before the day-trip crowds arrive, which is worth it if your schedule allows it but not essential otherwise.

Quick answers

A few follow-up questions travelers ask about this.

Can Chefchaouen be combined with a desert tour?

Not directly on our existing routes — it's in a different region of the country. It works best as a separate stop on a longer trip that also includes the north, typically added on to time spent in Fes.

Is one day in Chefchaouen enough?

Yes, for most travelers — a single day covers the medina, the main viewpoints and some shopping time comfortably; an overnight is a nice-to-have, not a necessity.

Why is Chefchaouen painted blue?

Several theories are commonly cited — symbolic, religious and practical — but the wide blue-washing seen today largely developed from the 1930s onward rather than being an unbroken ancient tradition.
