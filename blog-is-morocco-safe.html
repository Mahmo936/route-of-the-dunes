Is Morocco Safe to Travel To?

Not a generic reassurance — here's what the real risks actually are, and what genuinely isn't one.

8 min read · Planning

This question deserves a specific answer, not a reflexive "yes, totally fine!" — Morocco is a safe country to travel in by most reasonable measures, but "safe" isn't the same as "risk-free," and the actual risks worth knowing about aren't the ones most first-time visitors worry about.

What the real risk actually is: petty theft, not violent crime

Violent crime against tourists is rare in the parts of Morocco visitors actually go — Marrakesh, Fes, the desert route, the coast. The more realistic risk is petty theft: pickpocketing in crowded medina alleys and market squares, the odd overcharge from an unlicensed guide, or a taxi meter that mysteriously "isn't working." None of this is dangerous, but it's worth the same ordinary caution you'd use in any busy tourist city — a bag zipped shut, valuables not left visible, agreeing on taxi fares before you get in.

Road safety is the more genuine consideration

If there's one category worth taking seriously, it's road conditions rather than crime — mountain passes like the Tizi n'Tichka, shared roads with slower traffic, and long driving days. This is exactly why booking with an operator who uses licensed, experienced drivers on these specific roads matters more than almost anything else you'll check before booking a desert tour.

Scams: annoying, not dangerous

The most common negative experience travelers report isn't safety-related at all — it's low-grade tourist scams: unofficial "guides" who attach themselves uninvited, inflated prices for common goods, or a tour that turns out to include far less than promised. See our guide to avoiding tour scams for the specific red flags to check before you pay any deposit. Being scammed a few dirhams on an orange juice is a bad afternoon, not a safety incident — but it's worth knowing the difference.

How Morocco compares. On most independent safety indices, Morocco ranks similarly to — or safer than — several popular European city-break destinations for tourist-directed crime. The country sees millions of foreign visitors a year specifically because this reputation holds up in practice, not just in marketing.

Solo and female travelers specifically

This comes up often enough to deserve its own answer: solo travel, including for women, is common and generally safe on the well-traveled routes, provided you take the same precautions you would in any unfamiliar country. See our dedicated guide to solo female travel in the Sahara for what to actually check before booking with any operator.

What we'd actually tell a first-time visitor

  • Keep valuables out of sight in crowded medina areas, same as any busy city
  • Agree on taxi fares or insist on the meter before the ride starts
  • Book tours and guides through operators with verifiable reviews, not whoever approaches you on the street
  • For desert routes specifically, prioritize a licensed driver-guide over the cheapest possible price

Health and practical safety

No unusual vaccinations are required for most nationalities visiting Morocco, though it's worth checking current recommendations with a travel clinic or your own healthcare provider before you go, since guidance varies by country of origin and can change. Tap water is generally best avoided in favor of bottled water, and sun exposure — not an exotic illness — is the most common health issue visitors actually deal with, especially in the desert stretches of a tour. Basic travel insurance covering medical care and trip disruption is a sensible precaution for any international trip, this one included.

A few common misconceptions worth correcting

Some of what circulates online about Morocco overstates the actual risk. Being female, traveling solo, or visiting during Ramadan are all commonly framed as bigger obstacles than they are in practice — each is manageable with the ordinary preparation described above, not a reason to avoid the trip. Similarly, the presence of street vendors or unofficial guides approaching tourists in medina areas is a persistent nuisance, not a safety threat — a firm "no thank you" and continuing to walk resolves it in the overwhelming majority of cases.

Quick answers

A few follow-up questions travelers ask about this.

Is it safe to walk around Marrakesh at night?

Yes, in the main tourist areas and well-lit streets — use the same judgment you would in any city, and avoid poorly lit, empty alleys late at night.

Do I need to worry about food and water safety?

Stick to bottled water and busy, high-turnover restaurants, the same general advice that applies across most of North Africa and the Middle East; most travelers have no issues.

Is the desert route itself safe to drive?

Yes, with an experienced local driver-guide — this is exactly why the choice of operator matters more on mountain and desert roads than on a simple city taxi ride.
