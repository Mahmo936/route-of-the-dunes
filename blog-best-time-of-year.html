Best Time of Year to Visit the Sahara

Spring and autumn are the easy answer — here's what actually changes month to month, and when the trade-offs are still worth it.

6 min read · Planning

The short answer is spring (March–May) and autumn (September–November), but that's not the whole picture — the "off" months still work for the right traveler, and knowing what actually changes month to month helps you plan around it rather than just avoid it.

Spring and autumn: the easy months

Warm, comfortable days and cool (not cold) nights make these the most universally pleasant months for the whole route — driving days, kasbah stops, and the desert camp itself. This is also when demand is highest, so book a little further ahead if your dates are fixed.

Summer (June–August): hot, but manageable

Daytime temperatures climb well into the high 30s and 40s°C in the desert stretches, though the vehicle is air-conditioned for the driving portions. Evenings at the dunes cool down noticeably once the sun sets. Summer isn't the easiest season, but it's far from unbearable — early starts and a genuinely good sense of what you're walking into make it workable, and it's often the cheapest and least crowded time to go.

Winter (December–February): cold nights, clear skies

Days stay mild, but nights at Erg Chebbi can drop close to freezing — camps provide extra blankets and heaters, and packing a genuine warm layer matters more this season than any other. The trade-off: winter often has the clearest, driest skies of the year, which makes it a strong choice if stargazing or star photography is a priority for your trip.

What the numbers actually look like

To put rough figures on it: spring and autumn daytime highs in the desert typically sit in the high 20s to low 30s°C, dropping to a comfortable mid-teens overnight. Summer regularly pushes daytime highs past 40°C in the Sahara stretches specifically, though the High Atlas crossing earlier in the route stays noticeably cooler. Winter days often hover in the high teens to low 20s°C, with desert nights dropping to single digits or occasionally just below freezing.

The shoulder-season sweet spots

Late March and early October in particular tend to combine the best of both worlds — warm but not scorching days, cool but not freezing nights, and demand that hasn't quite hit its peak-season high. If your travel dates are flexible, aiming for the edges of the spring or autumn windows rather than their busiest middle weeks can mean slightly better prices and availability without giving up much in comfort.

Desert nights stay cool year-round. Even in peak summer, temperatures at Erg Chebbi drop noticeably after sunset — a light layer for the evening is worth packing regardless of which month you travel.

Planning around Ramadan specifically

Ramadan shifts roughly 10–11 days earlier each year on the Gregorian calendar, so it's worth checking the actual dates for your travel year rather than assuming. During the month, many restaurants in smaller towns along the route open only after sunset, though tourist-oriented restaurants and your tour's own included meals continue as normal. It's a genuinely interesting time to experience Moroccan culture, not a month to avoid, but worth knowing about in advance so mealtime expectations are set correctly.

Quick answers

A few follow-up questions travelers ask about this.

Is it ever too hot to do the tour at all?

No — even peak summer is manageable with early starts and an air-conditioned vehicle, though it's genuinely the most physically demanding season if heat bothers you.

What's the coldest it gets at the dunes in winter?

Nighttime temperatures can approach freezing in December and January, which is why camps provide extra blankets and heaters during these months.

Is spring or autumn better if I have to choose one?

Both are similarly comfortable; autumn (September–November) sometimes edges out spring slightly for clearer skies, but the difference is marginal.
