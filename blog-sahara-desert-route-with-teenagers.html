Sahara Desert Tours with Teens: Keeping Them Engaged

Teens need realistic expectations and a bit of autonomy to survive long road trips across Morocco.

6 min read · Travelers

The road from Marrakesh or Fes to the Erg Chebbi dunes covers serious ground. If you are traveling with teenagers, the hardest part of the trip isn't the heat or the sand—it is the sheer volume of hours spent inside a vehicle crossing the Atlas Mountains and the Ziz Valley.

We don't believe in overselling family desert trips. Teenagers get bored easily, they rely on Wi-Fi, and they have zero patience for forced family bonding. Here is an honest look at how to structure this route so everyone stays reasonably happy.

Managing the Long Driving Days

The first and last days of any desert itinerary involve significant driving—often six to eight hours with stops. For teenagers, this can feel endless. The best way to handle this is to establish expectations early: download offline entertainment before you leave the hotel, because mobile data drops out entirely once you hit the mountains and remote plateaus.

However, don't let them sleep through the entire drive with headphones on. Break up the day by stepping out at viewpoints, walking through the Todra Gorge, or exploring the kasbahs. Giving them a specific task—like picking the playlist for a stretch of the road or handling a small part of the route navigation—gives them a bit of control.

  • Download movies and music before leaving major cities
  • Bring a portable power bank for phones and tablets
  • Take every rest stop to stretch legs and use proper facilities

Wi-Fi, Connectivity, and Teen Anxiety

Let’s address the elephant in the room: disconnecting from social media can cause genuine stress for some teenagers. On the desert route, cell service is patchy at best. Most standard riads and desert camps have Wi-Fi, but it is often slow, unreliable, or entirely absent during sandstorms or remote stretches.

We recommend letting your teens know in advance where and when they will have internet access. Setting clear windows—such as 'you can check messages when we reach the hotel tonight, but keep phones away during the mountain crossings'—prevents constant arguments in the backseat.

Balancing Activity and Downtime

A common mistake is packing every afternoon with extra walking tours or historical lectures. Teenagers generally prefer physical activity or downtime over standing in the sun listening to dates. Balance the itinerary with things that actually engage them, like quad biking, sandboarding down the dunes, or walking the oasis paths independently.

When you arrive at the desert camp, give them time to explore the dunes on their own terms. Sunset photos, climbing the highest ridge, or just sitting quietly away from the group often appeals to older kids more than structured camp activities.

Food and Complainers

Moroccan cuisine is incredible, but it can be repetitive for picky eaters who face tagine and couscous at every roadside stop. On long travel days, grab some snacks, crisps, and fruit from local corner shops in the towns along the way to avoid low-blood-sugar meltdowns.

If your teenager is resistant to trying new foods, most standard tourist route restaurants also offer basic omelettes, fries, or brochettes. Don't fight every battle; sometimes a plate of plain grilled meat keeps the peace.

  • Stock up on familiar snacks at larger towns
  • Allow simple backup meal choices when available
  • Keep hydrated—dehydration causes unexpected mood drops

Quick answers

A few follow-up questions travelers ask about this.

Will my teenager get bored on the drive?

Probably at some point. The drives are long. Downloading offline entertainment, scheduling frequent stops, and bringing a power bank make a huge difference.

Is there Wi-Fi at the desert camps?

Most standard and luxury camps have limited Wi-Fi in the main dining tent, but speeds are slow and it often cuts out entirely.

Are desert activities like quad biking safe for older teens?

Yes, quad biking and sandboarding are very popular with teenagers. Always follow the local guide's safety instructions and wear the provided helmets.
