Sahara Tours for Less Active Travelers: What to Expect

An honest assessment of the sitting, riding, and walking involved in a Sahara desert journey for travelers with lower stamina.

6 min read · Planning

When you look at photos of the Erg Chebbi dunes, it is easy to focus entirely on the scenery: the copper-colored sand, the endless horizon, the quiet. But getting there and experiencing it involves physical actions—long hours in a vehicle, sitting atop a camel, and walking on shifting sand—that catch some travelers by surprise. We prefer to be upfront about the physical realities so you can plan accordingly, rather than finding out mid-trip that your lower back is stiff or your stamina is spent.

This guide breaks down the physical demands of each part of a standard 3-day or 4-day desert tour with routeofthedunes.com. Whether you are traveling with aging parents, recovering from an injury, or simply prefer a slower, less strenuous pace, understanding these trade-offs helps you choose the right options—such as taking a 4x4 to camp instead of riding a camel—before you leave Marrakesh or Fes.

The Long Drives Across the Atlas

The journey from Marrakesh or Fes to Merzouga requires crossing the Atlas Mountains and the pre-desert plateaus. This means spending consecutive hours in a vehicle—often 7 to 9 hours on the first and last days. The roads are paved and generally good, but the section over the Tizi n'Tichka pass features endless switchbacks that can test anyone prone to motion sickness.

For travelers with back issues or limited mobility, this is the most demanding part of the itinerary. We build in frequent stops for stretch breaks, coffee, and photos every hour and a half to two hours, but you will still spend a large portion of three days seated in a van or 4x4. If you need extra legroom, let us know in advance so we can factor that into your vehicle assignment.

  • Pack motion sickness tablets if you are sensitive to winding mountain roads
  • Bring a small lumbar pillow or rolled jacket for lower back support
  • Expect driving days to range from 5 to 9 hours depending on your start point

Camel Riding: Muscle Groups and Comfort

Riding a camel for the standard 60 to 90 minutes into the desert camp is iconic, but it is not a gentle armchair ride. Camels have a distinct, rolling gait that requires your core muscles and inner thighs to engage constantly to keep your balance. If you have hip stiffness, knee issues, or lower back pain, mounting and riding a camel can quickly become uncomfortable.

Furthermore, mounting and dismounting involve the camel rising and kneeling rather abruptly while you are perched high off the ground. There is no shame in skipping this part. We can arrange for a 4x4 vehicle to transport you directly to the desert camp with your luggage while the rest of the group rides, allowing you to enjoy the sunset without the physical strain.

  • Core and inner thigh muscles work harder than you might expect
  • Mounting and dismounting require decent upper body and leg stability
  • 4x4 transfers to camp are always available as an alternative to riding

Walking and Climbing on Sand

Walking on sand is fundamentally different from walking on firm ground. Every step sinks slightly, causing your ankles, calves, and Achilles tendons to work significantly harder than they would on a standard flat trail. Climbing even a modest dune can leave you breathing heavily within a few minutes, not necessarily due to altitude, but simply because of the shifting footing.

If you want to watch the sunrise or sunset from a high ridge, take it slow and steady. There is no rush, and you do not need to reach the highest peak to get a magnificent view. If you prefer to stay on flat, packed clay ground near the base camp or the Berber tent, you can still experience the stillness of the desert without putting stress on your joints.

  • Sand acts like a cushion that saps momentum with every stride
  • Ankles and calves absorb extra stabilizing load
  • Pacing yourself and stopping frequently makes dune viewing accessible

Adapting the Itinerary for Your Pace

A standard 3-day tour packs a massive amount of geography into a short window. If you are a less active traveler, the rushed transitions between hotels, sights, and the desert can feel draining. This is why we often recommend a slower pace—either by adding an extra day to your itinerary or by choosing a private tour rather than a shared group vehicle.

With a private booking, you dictate the schedule. If you need an extra twenty minutes at a roadside café, a slower morning departure, or a direct vehicle transfer past the camel trek, you can adjust the plan on the fly.   • Consider extending your trip duration to reduce daily driving hours
  • Private tours allow complete flexibility to skip strenuous activities

Quick answers

A few follow-up questions travelers ask about this.

Can I skip the camel ride and still reach the desert camp?

Yes. We regularly arrange for a 4x4 vehicle to drive travelers directly to the camp while the rest of the group rides camels. You arrive at the same time and enjoy the exact same evening.

How long are the actual driving days on a Marrakesh to Merzouga tour?

Days 1 and 3 involve roughly 7 to 9 hours in the car, broken up by lunch, viewpoints, and bathroom stops every hour and a half. Day 2 is generally much shorter since you are already in the desert region.

Is dune climbing required to enjoy the Sahara?

Not at all. The camp areas are set in flatter valleys between the dunes. You can sit outside your tent, drink mint tea, and watch the sunset without climbing a single grain of steep sand.
